Utopia For Realists Data

1. Human progress  
   1. For roughly 99% of the world’s history, 99% of humanity was poor, hungry, dirty, afraid, stupid, sick, and ugly. Blaise Pascal described life as one giant vat of human tears. In the last 200 years, billions of us have suddenly become rich, well nourished, clean, safe, smart, healthy, and occasionally even beautiful. Where 84% of the world’s population still lived in extreme poverty in 1820, by 1981 that percentage had dropped to 44%, and now, just a few decades later, it is under 10%.  
   2. Of course, even though our lives are just that much better than they used to be, that doesn’t mean they can’t get better.  
2. Money for the poor  
   1. The idea that poor people can’t handle money is just objectively false. Our society may be portrayed as a meritocracy where the only person you can blame for your failures is yourself, but the reality shows how many people are kicked out because the system itself is working against them. People aren’t usually poor because they can’t manage their money properly but because the system won’t let them manage their money properly. This idea also connects to the seeming truism that free money makes people lazy. However, according to the evidence, it just doesn’t.  
   2. GiveDirectly, an organization founded by Michael Faye, just gives money directly to the poor. The people with the best sense of what poor people need are the poor people themselves. According to a study by MIT, their cash grants spur a 38% lasting increase in incomes and also boost homeownership and possession of livestock by 58% while reducing the number of days that children go hungry by 42%. Research has time and time again correlated unconditional cash disbursements with reductions in crime, child mortality, malnutrition, teenage pregnancy, and truancy, while improving school performance, economic growth, and gender equality. As Charles Kenny states, the big reason poor people are poor is because they don’t have enough money.  
   3. The best policy implementation for this kind of giving is Universal Basic Income. No questions asked, just free money for everyone. Mincome showed it was plausible and effective, as did LBJ’s experiments in New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Iowa, North Carolina, Indiana, Seattle, and Denver. The opening of Harrah’s Cherokee and its impact on the Indigenous tribe also shows the extensive benefits and effectiveness of such a program, significantly improving the lives of everyone involved.  
3. GDP is kind of a bad measure  
   1. The specific nature of certain activities are not measured, and that’s really the most important part of the stats that GDP is used for.  
   2. It’s kind of used as a one size fits all measure for economic growth, but it’s incredibly crucial to know what and how exactly GDP measures that idea. Economic growth is a much more complicated concept than is able to be summed up by a single number, and remembering that there are both good and bad types of growth is important.  
4. More leisure is more good (and plausible)  
   1. The unit cost of production is so low that we can afford to pay as much for six hours as we formerly paid for eight. Kellog found that out when he introduced a six-hour workday at his factory in Battle Creek, Michigan. It was an unmitigated success as he was able to hire an additional 300 employees and slashed the accident rate by 41%. His employees became noticeably more productive, as the idea of diminishing returns holds true. A shorter workweek is simply a matter of good business, as Ford also saw in his own factories. British Prime Minister Edward Heath discovered that fact inadvertently when instating a three-day workweek during a lack of energy reserves and only seeing a 6% production loss.  
5. Some jobs are useless (and that’s a good thing)  
   1. If garbagemen were to strike, as seen in New York of 1968, the government would have to give in no matter what. The job is crucial to a city’s functioning, and that was made extremely apparent. On the opposite end, however, if Washington’s lobbyists of Manhattan’s accountants went on strike, no one would even notice. The difference between these jobs is that the former *creates wealth* while the latter just *shifts* it around. Of course, there’s no clear line. Lots of jobs do both and there’s no denying that the financial sector is a great example of that at times. However, these days, banks have become so big that much of what they do is merely shuffle wealth around, or even destroy it. Instead of growing the pie, the explosive expansion of the banking sector has increased the share it serves itself. For every dollar a bank earns, an estimated equivalent of 60 cents is destroyed elsewhere in the economy. Conversely, for every dollar a researcher earns, a value of at least $5, and often much more, is pumped back into the economy. Bizarrely, it’s precisely the jobs that shift money around, creating basically no tangible value, that net the best salaries. There seems to be a sort of conviction that you’re producing something of great value *because* you earn so much, and not the other way around.  
   2. One solution is taxes. For example, a transactions tax could rein in the financial industry. By paying a fee each time you buy or sell a stock, you would no longer gain from split-second buying and selling of financial assets. Higher taxes for top earners would serve to reallocate talented individuals from professions that cause negative externalities to those that cause positive externalities, in other words getting more people to do work that’s more useful.  
   3. Another is fixing education. Rather than trying to teach students the knowledge and skills they need to get hired in tomorrow’s job market, we should be teaching them the knowledge and skills we *want* them to have. Education is a way to *shift* the future rather than attempting to *predict* and *account* for it.  
6. Open Borders  
   1. An economist sees a $100 bill in the street. Being a rational person, he doesn’t pick it up, because how could it be anything but a fake?  
      1. The assumption that people are rational people, as the field of economics uses as its basis, is inherently inaccurate. This issue comes out incredibly well in the topic of foreign aid, where everyone has different ideas on how to go about fixing peoples’ lives through money. In theory, however, our ideas always end up working. Practice usually turns out different, as we can only find out through RCT’s and similar experiments on the ground. The best way to see if something works isn’t to rigorously theorize but to rigorously test.  
   2. Four different studies have shown that, depending on the level of movement in the global labor market, the estimated growth in “gross worldwide product” would be in the range of 67% to 147%. Effectively, open borders would make the whole world *twice* as rich.  
7. Extreme ideas are good  
   1. The Enlightenment model of how people change their opinions, through information-gathering and reasoned deliberation, is objectively wrong. People are most likely to change their opinions if you confront them with new and disagreeable facts as directly as possible, as James Kuklinski showed. A worldview is not a Lego set where a block is added here and removed there. It’s a fortress that is defended tooth and nail, with all possible reinforcements, until the pressure becomes so overpowering that the walls cave in.  
   2. We tend to conform to the group we want to belong to, as was found in Asch’s now-famous experiments. However, the less famous but perhaps more important finding was that a single opposing voice can make all the difference. When just one person stuck to the truth, the test subjects were more likely to trust the evidence of their own senses. Keep on building those castles in the sky, as your time will come.
